## 1. Double-Entry Accounting and the Accounting Equation

The fundamental accounting equation is the foundation of double-entry bookkeeping and must remain balanced at all times:
$$\\text{Assets} = \\text{Liabilities} + \\text{Equity}$$

Every business transaction impacts the components of this equation in a way that preserves this equilibrium. In financial accounting, we classify transactions into debits and credits and post them to ledger accounts based on their effect on this equation.

### A. Ramesh's Capital Investment
*   **Transaction:** Ramesh invests ₹200,000 in cash to start his business [452, 982].
*   **Business Entity Concept:** Under this concept, the business is completely distinct and separate from its owner [454, 984]. Therefore, any capital invested by the owner is considered a liability from the business's perspective, representing the amount the business owes back to the owner [454, 984].
*   **Impact on Accounting Equation:**
    $$\\text{Assets (Cash)} \\uparrow \\text{ by ₹200,000} \\quad \\text{and} \\quad \\text{Owner's Equity (Capital)} \\uparrow \\text{ by ₹200,000} \\quad \\text{[452, 982]}$$
*   **Equation State:**
    $$\\text{₹200,000 (Assets)} = \\text{₹0 (Liabilities)} + \\text{₹200,000 (Equity)}$$

### B. Paying Off Suppliers (Accounts Payable)
*   **Transaction:** A company has ₹10,000 in Accounts Payable and pays off ₹3,000 to its suppliers [450, 980].
*   **Impact on Accounting Equation:**
    $$\\text{Assets (Cash)} \\downarrow \\text{ by ₹3,000} \\quad \\text{and} \\quad \\text{Liabilities (Accounts Payable)} \\downarrow \\text{ by ₹3,000} \\quad \\text{[450, 980]}$$
*   **Equation State:** Both sides of the equation decrease by exactly ₹3,000, maintaining perfect balance:
    $$\\text{(Assets - ₹3,000)} = \\text{(Liabilities - ₹3,000)} + \\text{Equity}$$

### C. Purchasing a Delivery Truck for Cash
*   **Transaction:** A business purchases a new delivery truck for cash [453, 983].
*   **Impact on Accounting Equation:**
    $$\\text{Assets (Cash)} \\downarrow \\text{ by the truck's cost} \\quad \\text{and} \\quad \\text{Assets (Fixed Assets/Vehicles)} \\uparrow \\text{ by the truck's cost} \\quad \\text{[453, 983]}$$
*   **Total Assets Impact:** Total assets remain **unchanged** [453, 983]. One asset (Cash) is simply exchanged for another asset (Vehicles) of equal value, leaving the liabilities and equity sides completely unaffected.

### D. Recording Drawings
*   **Transaction:** A business owner (e.g., Rohan) takes ₹3,000 from his business for his personal house rent [451, 981].
*   **Impact on Accounting Equation:**
    $$\\text{Assets (Cash)} \\downarrow \\text{ by ₹3,000} \\quad \\text{and} \\quad \\text{Owner's Equity (Drawings)} \\downarrow \\text{ by ₹3,000} \\quad \\text{[451, 981]}$$
*   **Classification:** This is recorded as **'Drawings'**, which directly reduces the Owner's Equity [451, 981]. It is not recorded as a rent expense for the business, because the rent is for personal use, not business operations.

### E. Settlement of a Prior Month's Liability
*   **Transaction:** A business pays ₹5,000 for a utility bill that was recorded as a liability in the previous month [456, 986].
*   **Impact on current month's Income Statement:**
    *   There is **no impact on the current month's Income Statement** [456, 986].
*   **Accrual Accounting Explanation:** Under accrual accounting, the utility expense was already recognized on the Income Statement of the previous month (the period when the utility was consumed and the liability was recorded). The cash payment in the current month only reduces Cash (Asset) and reduces Accounts Payable / Outstanding Liabilities (Liability) on the Balance Sheet, leaving current-month revenues and expenses unaffected.

## 2. Trading Account, Cost of Goods Sold, and the Income Statement

The Income Statement summarizes a company's financial performance over an operating period by matching revenues against expenses.

### A. Gross Profit Calculation
Gross Profit represents the surplus generated from core manufacturing or trading activities before accounting for indirect overheads (such as administration, marketing, or interest expenses).
$$\\text{Gross Profit} = \\text{Sales Revenue} - \\text{Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)} \\quad \\text{[451, 981]}$$

### B. Trading Account Recording
The Trading Account is the initial section of the Income Statement where direct manufacturing or trading items are balanced.
*   **Credit Side:** **'Closing Stock'** and **'Sales'** are recorded on the **Credit Side** of the Trading Account [453, 983].
*   **Debit Side:** Opening Stock, Purchases, and direct manufacturing costs (such as direct wages or freight-in) are recorded on the Debit Side.


## 3. Inventory Valuation: Weighted Average Method

When a business holds stock purchased at different price points over time, it must apply a systematic inventory valuation method (such as FIFO, LIFO, or Weighted Average) to determine the value of materials consumed and closing stock.

### A. Weighted Average Cost Formula
$$\\text{Weighted Average Cost per Unit} = \\frac{\\text{Total Cost of Inventory}}{\\text{Total Number of Units in Inventory}}$$

### B. Numerical Example
*   **Scenario:** A business holds the following inventory purchases on its books [456, 986]:
    *   100 units purchased at ₹5 per unit
    *   200 units purchased at ₹8 per unit
*   **Step-by-Step Derivation:**
    1.  **Calculate the Total Cost of Inventory:**
        $$\\text{Total Cost} = (100 \\text{ units} \\times ₹5) + (200 \\text{ units} \\times ₹8)$$
        $$\\text{Total Cost} = ₹500 + ₹1,600 = ₹2,100 \\quad \\text{[456, 986]}$$
    2.  **Calculate the Total Number of Units:**
        $$\\text{Total Units} = 100 \\text{ units} + 200 \\text{ units} = 300 \\text{ units} \\quad \\text{[456, 986]}$$
    3.  **Compute the Weighted Average Cost per Unit:**
        $$\\text{Weighted Average Cost per Unit} = \\frac{₹2,100}{300 \\text{ units}} = ₹7.00 \\text{ per unit} \\quad \\text{[456, 986]}$$
*   **Outcome:** The average cost per unit to be used for pricing inventory consumption and closing stock is **₹7.00** [456, 986].


## 4. Depreciation Accounting Methods

Depreciation is the systematic and rational allocation of the cost of a tangible fixed asset over its estimated useful life. It represents the wear and tear of long-term assets used in business operations.

### A. Straight-Line Method (SLM)
Under the Straight-Line Method, depreciation is allocated equally across each year of the asset's useful life. The annual depreciation expense remains constant.

#### Straight-Line Formula:
$$\\text{Annual Depreciation Expense} = \\frac{\\text{Original Cost} - \\text{Estimated Salvage Value}}{\\text{Useful Life (in years)}} \\quad \\text{[457, 987]}$$

#### Numerical Case:
*   **Asset Purchase Cost:** ₹11,000 [457, 987]
*   **Estimated Salvage (Residual) Value:** ₹1,000 [457, 987]
*   **Estimated Useful Life:** 5 years [457, 987]
*   **Required:** Calculate the Book Value of the asset at the end of Year 2 [457, 987].

#### Step-by-Step Derivation:
1.  **Calculate the Annual Depreciation Expense:**
    $$\\text{Annual Depreciation} = \\frac{₹11,000 - ₹1,000}{5 \\text{ years}}$$
    $$\\text{Annual Depreciation} = \\frac{₹10,000}{5} = ₹2,000 \\text{ per year} \\quad \\text{[457, 987]}$$
2.  **Calculate the Accumulated Depreciation at the end of Year 2:**
    $$\\text{Accumulated Depreciation (End of Year 2)} = \\text{Annual Depreciation} \\times 2 \\text{ years}$$
    $$\\text{Accumulated Depreciation} = ₹2,000 \\times 2 = ₹4,000$$
3.  **Calculate the Net Book Value at the end of Year 2:**
    $$\\text{Book Value (End of Year 2)} = \\text{Original Cost} - \\text{Accumulated Depreciation}$$
    $$\\text{Book Value (End of Year 2)} = ₹11,000 - ₹4,000 = ₹7,000 \\quad \\text{[457, 987]}$$
*   **Outcome:** Under the Straight-Line Method, the book value of the asset at the end of Year 2 is **₹7,000** [457, 987].


### B. Written Down Value (WDV) Method
Under the Written Down Value (diminishing balance) method, depreciation is calculated as a fixed percentage on the diminishing book value (written down value) of the asset at the beginning of each year. The depreciation expense declines each year.

#### DIMINISHING BALANCE Depreciation Formula:
$$\\text{Depreciation Expense for Year } t = \\text{Book Value at Beginning of Year } t \\times \\text{Depreciation Rate}$$

#### Numerical Case:
*   **Asset Purchase Cost:** ₹10,000 [458, 988]
*   **Depreciation Rate:** 20% per annum (WDV method) [458, 988]
*   **Required:** Calculate the depreciation expense specifically for the second year [458, 988].

#### Step-by-Step Derivation:
1.  **Calculate the Depreciation Expense for Year 1:**
    $$\\text{Year 1 Depreciation} = \\text{Original Cost} \\times \\text{Depreciation Rate}$$
    $$\\text{Year 1 Depreciation} = ₹10,000 \\times 20\% = 0.20 \\times ₹10,000 = ₹2,000 \\quad \\text{[458, 988]}$$
2.  **Calculate the Written Down Value (Book Value) at the end of Year 1:**
    $$\\text{Book Value (End of Year 1)} = \\text{Original Cost} - \\text{Year 1 Depreciation}$$
    $$\\text{Book Value (End of Year 1)} = ₹10,000 - ₹2,000 = ₹8,000 \\quad \\text{[458, 988]}$$
    *Note: This Book Value of ₹8,000 is the opening base value for Year 2 calculations.*
3.  **Calculate the Depreciation Expense specifically for Year 2:**
    $$\\text{Year 2 Depreciation} = \\text{Book Value at Beginning of Year 2} \\times \\text{Depreciation Rate}$$
    $$\\text{Year 2 Depreciation} = ₹8,000 \\times 20\% = 0.20 \\times ₹8,000 = ₹1,600 \\quad \\text{[458, 988]}$$
*   **Outcome:** The depreciation expense specifically recorded for the second year is **₹1,600** [458, 988].

## 5. The Cash Flow Statement and Operating Cash Adjustments

The Cash Flow Statement tracking tracks the actual movement of cash into and out of a business, classified into three primary operational categories: Operating Activities, Investing Activities, and Financing Activities.

### A. Cash Flow Statement Classifications
1.  **Operating Activities:** Cash flows resulting from the principal revenue-generating activities of the enterprise, such as cash receipts from sales, payments to suppliers, and payment of operating expenses like utilities [21, 450].
2.  **Investing Activities:** Cash flows from the acquisition and disposal of long-term assets (such as land, buildings, machinery) and other investments not included in cash equivalents [15, 453].
    *   *Example Case:* If a company purchases a new factory building for cash, this transaction is classified on the Cash Flow Statement under **Investing Activities** [15, 453].
3.  **Financing Activities:** Cash flows resulting from transactions that alter the size and composition of equity capital and borrowings of the company, such as issuing shares, owner investments, owner withdrawals (drawings), or borrowing long-term loans [450, 451].

### B. Adjusting Net Income for Operating Cash Flow (Indirect Method)
In the indirect method of preparing the cash flow from Operating Activities, we start with Net Profit (Net Income) from the Income Statement and make adjustments for non-cash expenses and changes in working capital accounts.

#### The Depreciation Adjustment
*   **Rule:** Depreciation is **added back** to Net Income when calculating Cash Flow from Operating Activities [455, 985].
*   **Why is Depreciation Added Back?**
    *   Depreciation is a **non-cash expense** [455, 985].
    *   It reduced Net Income on the Income Statement (as a debit expense to match asset wear-and-tear), but did not involve any actual physical cash outflow from the business's bank accounts [455, 985].
    *   Adding it back to Net Income reverses the non-cash deduction to reveal the actual cash generated from operations [455, 985].
