# Week 7: Financial Ratio Analysis

## 1. Overview of Financial Ratio Analysis

Financial Ratio Analysis serves as a primary tool to translate raw financial statement numbers into meaningful, standardized, and actionable management insights [449]. By comparing relationships between different items on financial statements, ratio analysis enables business data analysts and managers to standardise evaluations across different companies, industry benchmarks, or time horizons [449].

Ratios are broadly classified into five core categories based on the specific operational or financial dimension they evaluate [1060]:
1.  **Liquidity Ratios:** Assess a firm's short-term solvency and ability to meet immediate liabilities [1060].
2.  **Solvency (Leverage) Ratios:** Measure long-term debt-carrying capacity and overall capital structure safety [1060].
3.  **Efficiency (Activity) Ratios:** Track how productively the company manages and utilizes its operational assets [1060].
4.  **Profitability Ratios:** Evaluate the company's financial returns relative to sales, assets, or equity [1060].
5.  **Market Valuation Ratios:** Measure the market value of the company's shares relative to its underlying accounting fundamentals [1060].

## 2. Liquidity Ratios (Short-Term Solvency)

Liquidity ratios focus on the current assets and current liabilities of a firm to determine if it has sufficient liquid cash or cash-equivalents to settle its obligations coming due within one year [10, 16].

### A. Current Ratio
The Current Ratio measures a firm's basic short-term liquid safety net. It compares total current assets directly against total current liabilities [10]:
$$\text{Current Ratio} = \frac{\text{Total Current Assets}}{\text{Total Current Liabilities}} \quad \text{[10]}$$

#### Strategic Interpretation and Industry Benchmarks:
*   An industry average of **2:1** is typically considered healthy, indicating that the business carries twice as many short-term resources as short-term debts [18].
*   If a company operates with a Current Ratio of **1:1** when the industry standard is 2:1, it implies that the business is carrying very tight liquidity [19].
*   **Risk Concern:** The business may face severe difficulty honoring its short-term obligations if inventory sales slow down or trade collections stall [19].
*   **Counter-argument (Efficiency):** Some managers might argue that a lower ratio is more efficient because it avoids carrying "lazy" or unproductive cash [19]. However, from a safety and lending perspective, a ratio significantly below the industry average poses a high liquidity risk [19].

## 3. Solvency Ratios (Long-Term Solvency)

Solvency ratios assess whether a business has the structural stability to honor its long-term debt and interest obligations over multiple years [14, 16].

### A. Debt-Equity Ratio
The Debt-Equity ratio measures a company's leverage and compares the total interest-bearing debt against the owners' equity [10, 14]:
$$\text{Debt-Equity Ratio} = \frac{\text{Total Debt (Borrowings)}}{\text{Total Shareholder's Equity}} \quad \text{[2]}$$

#### Strategic Interpretation:
*   A Debt-Equity ratio of **4:1** suggests that the company is heavily reliant on borrowed funds, with four times more debt capital than owner's equity capital [14].
*   **Risk for Lenders:** To potential lenders, a 4:1 ratio indicates **high financial risk** [14]. In periods of economic downturns, the high fixed debt-servicing obligations could easily push the firm into insolvency [14].

### B. Interest Coverage Ratio (ICR) / Interest Earned Ratio
The Interest Coverage Ratio measures how easily a firm can pay interest expenses on its outstanding debt from its cash-generating operational profits before non-cash adjustments [16, 21]. Under the standard curriculum methodology, depreciation is added back to the Profit After Tax (PAT) to evaluate cash availability [16]:
$$\text{Interest Coverage Ratio (ICR)} = \frac{\text{Profit After Tax (PAT)} + \text{Depreciation}}{\text{Interest on Long-term Debt}} \quad \text{[16, 21]}$$

#### Operational Logic:
*   **Why Depreciation is Added Back:** Depreciation is a non-cash expense [16]. While depreciation reduces net income on the Income Statement to account for the wearing out of assets over time, it does not involve any actual cash outflow from the bank [16, 455]. Therefore, this non-cash expense must be added back because it remains fully available as cash to pay the interest obligations [16, 455].

#### Numerical Practice Case:
*   **Given Data:** Profit After Tax (PAT) = ₹1,20,000; Interest on Long-Term Debt = ₹30,000; Depreciation = ₹50,000 [21].
*   **Calculation:**
    $$\text{Numerator (Cash available before depreciation)} = \text{PAT} + \text{Depreciation} = ₹1,20,000 + ₹50,000 = ₹1,70,000 \quad \text{[21, 22]}$$
    $$\text{ICR} = \frac{₹1,70,000}{₹30,000} = 5.67 \text{ times} \quad \text{[22]}$$
*   **Outcome:** The company's operational cash profit covers its interest payments **5.67 times** over, indicating a strong short-term capacity to service its debt [22].

## 4. Efficiency and Activity Ratios

Efficiency ratios evaluate how quickly and productively a business turns its operational balance-sheet assets (such as inventory and receivables) into revenue [17, 19].

### A. Debtors (Trade Receivables) Turnover Ratio
The Debtors Turnover Ratio measures how many times a year a business successfully collects its average trade receivables or outstanding customer invoices [3]:
$$\text{Debtors Turnover Ratio} = \frac{\text{Revenue from Operations}}{\text{Average (or Year-end) Debtors}} \quad \text{[3]}$$

### B. Inventory Turnover Ratio
The Inventory Turnover Ratio assesses how productively a company manages its inventory levels to avoid overstocking slow-moving or obsolete items [17]. It measures how many times the company's average stock of goods is processed and sold during a year [4]:
$$\text{Inventory Turnover Ratio} = \frac{\text{Cost of Materials Consumed (or COGS)}}{\text{Inventory}} \quad \text{[4]}$$
*   **Managerial Significance:** This ratio is highly useful for a manager trying to determine if the company is overstocking items that are not selling [17]. A declining ratio indicates that cash is being trapped in slow-moving stock [17].

### C. Inventory Holding Period
The Inventory Holding Period measures the average duration (in months or days) that stock remains sitting in warehouses before being sold [21]:
$$\text{Inventory Holding Period (Months)} = \frac{\text{Inventory}}{\text{Total Sales}} \times 12 \quad \text{[21]}$$
*   *Alternatively expressed as:* $12 / \text{Inventory Turnover Ratio}$.

#### Numerical Practice Case:
*   **Given Data:** Total Sales = ₹4,00,000; Inventory = ₹80,000 [21].
*   **Calculation:**
    $$\text{Inventory Holding Period} = \left( \frac{₹80,000}{₹4,00,000} \right) \times 12 \text{ months} = 0.20 \times 12 = 2.4 \text{ months} \quad \text{[21]}$$
*   **Outcome:** The average item of inventory remains in storage for **2.4 months** before being successfully cleared [21].

### D. Total Asset Turnover Ratio
Measures the revenue generated per rupee of assets owned by the business:
$$\text{Total Asset Turnover Ratio} = \frac{\text{Revenue from Operations}}{\text{Total Assets}} \quad \text{[4]}$$

#### Strategic Interpretation (Dupont Perspective):
*   If a company reports a healthy Net Profit Margin of 15% but exhibits a **very low Total Asset Turnover**, it indicates that the company is likely over-invested in assets that are not generating sufficient sales [20].
*   Even though individual transactions are highly profitable, the assets are underutilized, dragging down the overall Return on Equity (ROE) and capital efficiency [20].

## 5. Profitability Ratios

Profitability ratios measure the overall earning power and financial success of a firm relative to its operational scale or capital base [1, 2].

### A. Net Profit Margin
Measures the percentage of operational revenue that translates into net bottom-line earnings after all operating expenses, finance costs, depreciation, and taxes have been subtracted [1]:
$$\text{Net Profit Margin (\%)} = \left( \frac{\text{Profit After Tax (PAT)}}{\text{Revenue from Operations}} \right) \times 100\% \quad \text{[1, 3]}$$

### B. Return on Assets (ROA)
Evaluates how efficiently a company's total assets are being deployed to generate bottom-line profits [3]:
$$\text{Return on Assets (ROA)} = \left( \frac{\text{Profit After Tax (PAT)}}{\text{Total Assets}} \right) \times 100\% \quad \text{[3]}$$

### C. Return on Equity (ROE)
Measures the rate of return generated specifically on the capital invested by the equity shareholders [3]:
$$\text{Return on Equity (ROE)} = \left( \frac{\text{Profit After Tax (PAT)}}{\text{Total Shareholder's Equity}} \right) \times 100\% \quad \text{[3]}$$

## 6. Market Valuation Ratios

Market valuation ratios bridge the gap between internal accounting books and external stock market realities, helping investors determine if a share is fairly valued, overvalued, or undervalued [2, 17].

### A. Earnings Per Share (EPS)
EPS represents the portion of a company's profit allocated to each outstanding share of common stock [4]:
$$\text{EPS} = \frac{\text{Profit After Tax (PAT)}}{\text{Number of Outstanding Equity Shares}} \quad \text{[4]}$$

### B. Price-to-Earnings (P/E) Ratio
The P/E Ratio measures the relationship between the market price of a stock and its current earnings per share [4]:
$$\text{P/E Ratio} = \frac{\text{Current Market Price per Share}}{\text{Earnings Per Share (EPS)}} \quad \text{[4]}$$

#### Strategic Interpretation:
*   A P/E ratio of **25** indicates that investors are willing to pay **₹25 for every ₹1 of the company's current earnings** [18].
*   A high P/E ratio suggests that the market expects high future growth and expansion, prompting investors to pay a premium today [18]. Conversely, a low P/E ratio might signal that the firm is undervalued or facing operational bottlenecks [18].

#### Numerical Practice Case:
*   **Given Data:** Market Price = ₹120 per share; Profit After Tax (PAT) = ₹5,00,000; Outstanding Equity Shares = 50,000 [22].
*   **Step-by-Step Calculation:**
    1.  **Calculate Earnings Per Share (EPS):**
        $$\text{EPS} = \frac{\text{PAT}}{\text{Outstanding Shares}} = \frac{₹5,00,000}{50,000 \text{ shares}} = ₹10.00 \text{ per share} \quad \text{[22]}$$
    2.  **Calculate P/E Ratio:**
        $$\text{P/E Ratio} = \frac{\text{Market Price per Share}}{\text{EPS}} = \frac{₹120.00}{₹10.00} = 12 \quad \text{[22]}$$
*   **Outcome:** The company's P/E Ratio is **12** [22], meaning investors are paying ₹12 for every ₹1 of current annual earnings.

### C. Dividend Yield
Dividend Yield measures the direct cash return on investment provided to shareholders, expressed as a percentage of the current market price [4]:
$$\text{Dividend Yield (\%)} = \left( \frac{\text{Dividend Declared per Share}}{\text{Current Market Price per Share}} \right) \times 100\% \quad \text{[4]}$$

## 7. Case Study: Comprehensive Analysis of XYZ Limited

#### Additional Market Information:
*   **Current Market Price per Share:** ₹150.00 [2]
*   **Dividend Declared per Share:** ₹2.00 [2]
*   **Outstanding Shares:** Since the face value of each share is ₹1, the total number of outstanding equity shares is equal to the Equity Share Capital, which is **500 Crores** shares [2].

### B. Step-by-Step Derivation of Key Metrics

#### Question 1: Net Profit Margin
*   **Formula:** Net Profit Margin = (PAT / Revenue) * 100%
*   **Derivation:**
    $$\text{Net Profit Margin} = \left( \frac{3,375 \text{ Crores}}{40,000 \text{ Crores}} \right) \times 100\% = 0.084375 \times 100\% = 8.44\% \quad \text{[3]}$$
*   **Outcome:** Net Profit Margin is **8.44%** [3].

#### Question 2: Return on Assets (ROA)
*   **Formula:** ROA = (PAT / Total Assets) * 100%
*   **Derivation:**
    $$\text{ROA} = \left( \frac{3,375 \text{ Crores}}{30,000 \text{ Crores}} \right) \times 100\% = 0.1125 \times 100\% = 11.25\% \quad \text{[3]}$$
*   **Outcome:** ROA is **11.25%** [3].

#### Question 3: Return on Equity (ROE)
*   **Formula:** ROE = (PAT / Total Shareholder's Equity) * 100%
*   **Derivation:**
    $$\text{ROE} = \left( \frac{3,375 \text{ Crores}}{12,500 \text{ Crores}} \right) \times 100\% = 0.27 \times 100\% = 27.00\% \quad \text{[3]}$$
*   **Outcome:** ROE is **27.00%** [3].

#### Question 4: Debtors Turnover Ratio
*   **Assumption:** Average debtors equal year-end debtors (₹4,000 Crores) [3].
*   **Formula:** Debtors Turnover Ratio = Revenue from Operations / Debtors
*   **Derivation:**
    $$\text{Debtors Turnover Ratio} = \frac{40,000 \text{ Crores}}{4,000 \text{ Crores}} = 10.00 \text{ times} \quad \text{[3]}$$
*   **Outcome:** Debtors Turnover is **10.00 times** [3].

#### Question 5: Dividend Yield
*   **Formula:** Dividend Yield = (Dividend Declared per Share / Market Price per Share) * 100%
*   **Derivation:**
    $$\text{Dividend Yield} = \left( \frac{₹2.00}{₹150.00} \right) \times 100\% = 0.01333 \times 100\% = 1.33\% \quad \text{[4]}$$
*   **Outcome:** Dividend Yield is **1.33%** [4].

#### Question 6: Asset Turnover Ratio
*   **Formula:** Asset Turnover Ratio = Revenue from Operations / Total Assets
*   **Derivation:**
    $$\text{Asset Turnover Ratio} = \frac{40,000 \text{ Crores}}{30,000 \text{ Crores}} = 1.33 \text{ times} \quad \text{[4]}$$
*   **Outcome:** Asset Turnover is **1.33 times** [4].

#### Question 7: Inventory Turnover Ratio
*   **Formula:** Inventory Turnover Ratio = Cost of Materials Consumed / Inventory [4]
*   **Derivation:**
    $$\text{Inventory Turnover Ratio} = \frac{28,000 \text{ Crores}}{3,500 \text{ Crores}} = 8.00 \text{ times} \quad \text{[4]}$$
*   **Outcome:** Inventory Turnover is **8.00 times** [4].

#### Question 8: Earnings Per Share (EPS)
*   **Formula:** EPS = PAT / Number of Outstanding Equity Shares
*   **Derivation:**
    $$\text{EPS} = \frac{3,375 \text{ Crores of PAT}}{500 \text{ Crores of outstanding shares}} = ₹6.75 \text{ per share} \quad \text{[4]}$$
*   **Outcome:** EPS is **₹6.75** [4].

#### Question 9: Price to Earnings (P/E) Ratio
*   **Formula:** P/E Ratio = Market Price per Share / EPS
*   **Derivation:**
    $$\text{P/E Ratio} = \frac{₹150.00}{₹6.75} = 22.22 \quad \text{[4, 5]}$$
*   **Outcome:** P/E Ratio is **22.22** [5].

#### Question 10: Debt-Equity Ratio
*   **Formula:** Debt-Equity Ratio = Total Debt / Total Shareholder's Equity
*   **Derivation:**
    $$\text{Debt-Equity Ratio} = \frac{2,500 \text{ Crores}}{12,500 \text{ Crores}} = 0.20 \quad \text{[5]}$$
*   **Outcome:** Debt-Equity Ratio is **0.20** [5].

#### Question 11: EBITDA (Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortization)
*   **Formula:** EBITDA = Profit Before Tax (PBT) + Depreciation + Finance Costs (Interest)
*   **Derivation:**
    $$\text{EBITDA} = 4,500 \text{ Crores} + 800 \text{ Crores} + 200 \text{ Crores} = 5,500 \text{ Crores} \quad \text{[5]}$$
*   **Outcome:** EBITDA is **₹5,500 Crores** [5].

#### Question 12: Total Operating Expenses (excluding depreciation and finance costs)
*   **Formula:** Total Operating Expenses = Total Expenses - Depreciation - Finance Costs
*   **Derivation:**
    $$\text{Total Operating Expenses} = 35,500 \text{ Crores} - 800 \text{ Crores} - 200 \text{ Crores} = 34,500 \text{ Crores} \quad \text{[5]}$$
*   **Alternative Check:** Cost of Materials (28,000) + Employee Benefits (2,500) + Other Expenses (4,000) = 34,500 Crores [1].
*   **Outcome:** Total Operating Expenses (excl. depreciation and interest) are **₹34,500 Crores** [5].
