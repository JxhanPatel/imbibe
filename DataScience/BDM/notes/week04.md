## 4.1: Core Demand and Supply Mechanics

### 1. The Progression from Needs to Demand
Understanding consumer behavior requires distinguishing between four key psychological and economic concepts: needs, wants, desires, and demand.

*   **Need:** Essential requirements for human survival and well-being. Examples include food, clothing, shelter, and healthcare. A human being cannot survive without these basic requirements.
*   **Want:** Once basic needs are met, human aspirations rise. Wants enhance the quality of life and include things like entertainment and travel.
*   **Desire:** Driven primarily by emotions. The key difference between a want and a desire is a form of affordability. A consumer may wish or desire to buy certain items but lacks the necessary funds.
*   **Demand:** Desire backed by both the ability and willingness to pay. In economics, demand is defined as "effective demand." A person from a low-income group who wants to buy a luxury car does not constitute "demand" for the manufacturer because they lack purchasing power.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Anyone without purchasing power cannot demand a good or service. Demand must always be backed by the purchasing power of the consumers.

### 2. The Law of Demand
The Law of Demand states that, ceteris paribus (other things remaining the same), there is an inverse relationship between the price of a good and its quantity demanded. 
*   When the price of a particular good or service rises, the quantity demanded falls.
*   When the price of a particular good or service falls, the quantity demanded rises.

#### Key Terminology:
*   **Demand Schedule:** A tabular representation displaying the relationship between different price points and the corresponding quantity demanded.
*   **Demand Curve:** A graphical representation of the demand schedule, showing the price on the Y-axis and the quantity demanded on the X-axis. 
*   **Downward Slope:** The demand curve is downward sloping. This is because consumers are willing and able to purchase a smaller quantity of a good as its price increases.

#### Individual vs. Market Demand:
*   **Individual Demand:** The quantity of a good that a single consumer is willing and able to buy at various price points.
*   **Market Demand:** The sum total of all individual demands for a product or service. It is derived by horizontally summing up the quantities demanded by every individual consumer in the market at each given price level.

### 3. The Law of Supply
Supply represents the quantity of a good or service that sellers are willing and able to provide or offer for sale at different prices during a given period of time.
*   **Willingness to Sell:** Sellers must be motivated by the underlying profit they can generate from the sale.
*   **Ability to Sell:** Sellers must have the capacity or resources to produce the good. For example, if a factory's maximum capacity is 10,000 units, it cannot supply more than 10,000 units regardless of how high the price rises.

The Law of Supply states that, ceteris paribus (other things remaining the same), there is a direct (positive) relationship between price and quantity supplied.
*   When the price of a good rises, the quantity supplied also rises (as selling becomes more profitable, prompting and motivating sellers to produce and sell more).
*   When the price of a good falls, the quantity supplied also falls (as profits are squeezed or losses are incurred).

#### Key Terminology:
*   **Supply Schedule:** A tabular representation showing the relationship between pricing and quantity supplied.
*   **Supply Curve:** A graphical representation of the supply schedule. The supply curve is upward sloping, reflecting that higher prices incentivize sellers to offer larger quantities for sale.

*   **Market Supply:** The aggregate of all individual suppliers or sellers together that form the market.

### 4. Market Equilibrium
Market Equilibrium is the state where the market clears. This occurs at the price level where the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied. At this intersection, both buyers and sellers are balanced.

*   **Equilibrium Price:** The price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied.
*   **Equilibrium Quantity:** The quantity bought and sold at the equilibrium price.

#### Market Deviations from Equilibrium:
1.  **Surplus (Excess Supply):** Occurs when the market price is above the equilibrium price. Because the price is high, quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded. Sellers find themselves with unsold inventory. They respond by cutting prices, which stimulates demand and reduces supply, eventually bringing the price back down to the equilibrium level.
2.  **Shortage (Excess Demand):** Occurs when the market price is below the equilibrium price. Because the price is low, quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied. Sellers are not motivated to supply at this low price, and buyers want more. Sellers respond by raising the price, which reduces the shortage and brings the market back to equilibrium.

> [!NOTE]
> In a free market scenario, the price of any good naturally adjusts to bring the quantity supplied and the quantity demanded into a state of balance. This is the natural law of demand and supply.

### 5. Shifts of the Curve vs. Movements Along the Curve
It is critical to distinguish between a change in quantity demanded/supplied (movement along the curve) and a change in demand/supply (shift of the curve).

#### A. Demand Dynamics
*   **Movement Along the Demand Curve:** Triggered solely by a change in the price of the good itself. If the price increases, there is an upward movement along the curve (decrease in quantity demanded). If the price decreases, there is a downward movement along the curve (increase in quantity demanded).
*   **Shift of the Demand Curve:** Triggered by a change in any variable other than the price of the good itself that affects the buyer's desire to purchase.
    *   **Rightward Shift:** Represents an increase in demand (higher quantity demanded at every price point).
    *   **Leftward Shift:** Represents a decrease in demand (lower quantity demanded at every price point).

#### Key Variables Shifting the Demand Curve:
1.  **Income Levels:**
    *   *Normal Goods:* An increase in income leads to an increase in demand (rightward shift).
    *   *Inferior Goods:* An increase in income leads to a decrease in demand (leftward shift). For example, a consumer might take the bus instead of buying a car; here, the bus ride is an inferior good.
2.  **Prices of Related Goods:**
    *   *Substitutes:* Goods that can be used in place of one another (e.g., ice cream vs. frozen yogurt). A fall in the price of one reduces the demand for the other.
    *   *Complementary Goods:* Goods that are consumed together (e.g., ice cream and hot fudge). A fall in the price of one increases the demand for the other.
3.  **Preferences and Tastes:** Changes in trends or mindsets shift demand. For example, a fitness trend increases the demand for protein shakes.
4.  **Future Expectations:** If consumers expect prices to rise in the future (e.g., due to geopolitical tensions impacting fuel), they will increase current purchases, shifting the demand curve to the right.
5.  **Buyer Segment Size:** An increase in the number of buyers (e.g., a factory opening and employing 2,000 workers in a locality) causes a rightward shift in demand for local consumer products.

##### Public Policy Case Study: Cigarette Smoking
To discourage smoking, policymakers can employ two distinct economic strategies:
*   **Shifting the Demand Curve:** By using public announcements, advertising the bad health effects, and educating consumers, the public's taste changes. This causes a structural shift in the demand curve to the left. The price remains unchanged, but the quantity demanded decreases.
*   **Movement Along the Curve:** By levying high taxes on cigarette manufacturing, the input prices rise, forcing sellers to increase the price. The price rises (e.g., from 2 to 4), resulting in a movement along the same demand curve, which reduces the quantity demanded (e.g., from 20 to 12).

#### B. Supply Dynamics
*   **Movement Along the Supply Curve:** Triggered solely by a change in the price of the good itself.
*   **Shift of the Supply Curve:** Triggered by variables other than the price of the good itself.
    *   **Rightward Shift:** Represents an increase in supply.
    *   **Leftward Shift:** Represents a decrease in supply.

#### Key Variables Shifting the Supply Curve:
1.  **Input Prices:** An increase in the price of raw materials or labor increases the cost of production, making the product less profitable at the current selling price, shifting supply to the left.
2.  **Technological Advancements:** Process enhancements and automation reduce labor costs, improve yield, and scale up production, shifting the supply curve to the right.
3.  **Future Expectations:** If a firm expects the market price to rise in the future, it may store current production in inventory to sell later at a higher price, temporarily shifting the current supply curve to the left.
4.  **Number of Sellers:** If sellers leave the industry, total production capacity drops, shifting the supply curve to the left.

##  2: Elasticity of Demand and Supply

Elasticity measures the responsiveness of one variable to changes in another. In economics, it defines the degree of responsiveness of demand or supply to a change in price.

### 1. Price Elasticity of Demand (PED)
Price Elasticity of Demand (PED) measures the responsiveness of the quantity demanded of a good to a change in its price.

#### Formula:

*Example:* If a 10% increase in the price of an ice cream cone results in a 20% decrease in the quantity demanded:
$$PED = \frac{20\%}{10\%} = 2.0$$
Since $2.0 > 1$, the demand is classified as highly elastic.

#### Five Classifications of Demand Elasticity:
1.  **Elastic Demand ($PED > 1$):** The percentage change in quantity demanded is larger than the percentage change in price. Consumers are highly responsive to price changes (e.g., branded clothing, restaurants).
2.  **Inelastic Demand ($PED < 1$):** The percentage change in quantity demanded is smaller than the percentage change in price. Consumers are relatively insensitive to price changes (e.g., petrol, critical medicines).
3.  **Unit Elastic Demand ($PED = 1$):** The percentage change in quantity demanded is exactly equal to the percentage change in price (e.g., a 10% fall in price leads to a 10% rise in demand).
4.  **Perfectly Elastic Demand ($PED = \infty$):** Any tiny increase in price causes the quantity demanded to fall to zero. This occurs in highly competitive, commoditized markets with infinite substitutes.
5.  **Perfectly Inelastic Demand ($PED = 0$):** The quantity demanded remains constant regardless of the change in price. This applies to indispensable, life-saving items with no substitutes, such as insulin for diabetic patients.

<img width="1200" height="675" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/bd6cf310-89a7-43a0-a704-f3357ac7c536" />

#### Determinants of Demand Elasticity:
*   **Availability of Close Substitutes:** Goods with close substitutes have more elastic demand because consumers can easily switch brands (e.g., butter). Goods with no close substitutes have inelastic demand (e.g., eggs).
*   **Necessity vs. Luxury:** Necessities have inelastic demand (e.g., doctor visits), while luxuries have highly elastic demand (e.g., luxury yachts).
*   **Definition of the Market:** Narrowly defined markets have more elastic demand because it is easier to find substitutes (e.g., vanilla ice cream). Broadly defined markets have inelastic demand because substitutes are hard to find (e.g., food in general).
*   **Time Horizon:** Demand is more elastic over longer periods because consumers have time to adapt and find substitutes (e.g., switching to electric vehicles over time in response to high petrol prices).

### 2. Elasticity and Total Revenue
Total Revenue ($TR$) is the total amount of money a firm receives from selling its products:
$$TR = \text{Price } (P) \times \text{Quantity } (Q)$$

The price elasticity of demand directly determines the revenue implication of a price change:
*   **Elastic Demand ($PED > 1$):** A price increase results in a decrease in total revenue. This occurs because the percentage drop in quantity demanded is larger than the percentage increase in price, causing the product $P \times Q$ to fall.
    *   *Numerical Example:* A company sells 1,000 units at ₹100 each ($TR = ₹1,00,000$). It raises the price to ₹120 (a 20% increase), and sales volume drops to 700 units (a 30% drop). 
    *   $PED = 30\% / 20\% = 1.5$ (Elastic).
    *   New Revenue $= 700 \times ₹120 = ₹84,000$. Revenue fell from ₹1,00,000 to ₹84,000.
*   **Inelastic Demand ($PED < 1$):** A price increase results in an increase in total revenue. This occurs because the percentage drop in quantity demanded is smaller than the percentage increase in price, causing the product $P \times Q$ to rise.
*   **Unit Elastic Demand ($PED = 1$):** Total revenue remains constant when the price changes.

### 3. Price Elasticity of Supply (PES)
Price Elasticity of Supply (PES) measures the responsiveness of the quantity supplied of a good to a change in its price.

#### Formula:
$$PES = \frac{\% \text{ Change in Quantity Supplied}}{\% \text{ Change in Price}}$$

#### Five Classifications of Supply Elasticity:
1.  **Elastic Supply ($PES > 1$):** A small change in price leads to a substantial change in the quantity supplied (e.g., clothing manufacturers during a festive season who are not resource-constrained).
2.  **Inelastic Supply ($PES < 1$):** A change in price leads to a relatively small change in the quantity supplied. Production is difficult to shift quickly (e.g., agricultural crops in the short term).
3.  **Unit Elastic Supply ($PES = 1$):** The percentage change in supply equals the percentage change in price.
4.  **Perfectly Elastic Supply ($PES = \infty$):** Suppliers are willing to supply any quantity at a fixed price, but none at a lower price (e.g., commodity trading on thin margins).
5.  **Perfectly Inelastic Supply ($PES = 0$):** Supply remains completely constant regardless of the price. The quantity supplied cannot be increased (e.g., rare paintings by a deceased artist, or land supply on an isolated island).

#### Determinants of Supply Elasticity:
*   **Time Horizon:** Supply is highly inelastic in the short run (as capacity and resources are fixed) but becomes more elastic in the long run as firms build new factories, hire workers, and adjust production.
*   **Capacity Utilization:** If a factory has high unused capacity (operating at 40-50%), it can quickly increase production, making supply elastic. If it is already operating at its limit (95-98% capacity utilization), supply becomes highly inelastic because it cannot produce more units.
*   **Availability of Inputs:** If raw materials and labor are easily accessible, supply is elastic. If skilled labor or key materials are difficult to obtain, supply becomes inelastic.
*   **Nature of the Product:** Perishable goods (e.g., fresh fruits and vegetables) have inelastic supply because they must be sold quickly and cannot be stored.
*   **Technological Capability:** Automation and advanced technology allow firms to scale up production quickly, increasing supply elasticity.
*   **Government Regulation:** Highly regulated industries (e.g., mining with licensing restrictions and long gestation periods) have lower supply elasticity.

### 4. Economies of Scale and Economies of Scope
Profitability and supply-side capacity are heavily shaped by scale and scope:
*   **Economies of Scale:** Occurs when the average cost of production falls as the quantity of output increases. This is because fixed costs are spread over a larger number of units. This gives the supplier a competitive advantage and shifts the supply curve to the right, enabling bulk discounts.
*   **Economies of Scope:** Occurs when a firm can produce a wide variety of products more cheaply by sharing resources (e.g., machinery, personnel, or infrastructure) across those products, reducing total production costs.

### 5. Historical Case Studies in Elasticity

#### Case Study A: The Paradox of Public Policy in Agriculture
Suppose a new scientific invention allows farmers to significantly increase their wheat crop yields. This technological advancement shifts the supply curve of wheat to the right. 

Because wheat is a basic food commodity, its demand is highly inelastic. When the supply increases while demand remains unchanged, a surplus is created, causing the equilibrium price of wheat to fall significantly. Since the price falls drastically and the quantity sold only increases slightly (due to inelastic demand), the total revenue of the farmers actually decreases:
$$\text{Total Revenue } \downarrow = \text{Price } (\downarrow\downarrow) \times \text{Quantity } (\uparrow)$$

This represents an economic paradox: a productive harvest (good news for technology) results in lower revenue for the farmers (bad news). This explains why some public policies pay farmers not to plant crops to restrict supply and keep prices and revenues stable.

#### Case Study B: The OPEC Oil Crisis of the 1970s
In the 1970s, the Organization of Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC) decided to reduce the global supply of oil to increase prices and their overall incomes.

*   **The Short-Run Dynamics:** In the short run, both the supply and demand for oil were highly inelastic. Supply was inelastic because oil reserves and extraction capacity could not be changed overnight. Demand was inelastic because consumers could not immediately change their driving habits or heating systems. Because both curves were inelastic, the leftward shift in supply led to a massive, dramatic spike in oil prices, filling the coffers of OPEC countries.
*   **The Long-Run Dynamics:** Over the long run, both curves became much more elastic. Non-OPEC countries responded to high prices by investing in oil exploration and building extraction capacity (increasing supply elasticity). Consumers slowly adjusted by conserving energy, shifting to alternative sources, and replacing old, inefficient cars with fuel-efficient models (increasing demand elasticity). Consequently, the long-run curves smoothed out, and the price of oil steadily declined in the 1980s.

## 4.3: Consumer Behavior and Utility Theory

Consumer behavior theory explains how individuals allocate their limited income among different goods and services to maximize satisfaction.

### 1. The Concept of Utility
In economics, utility refers to the satisfaction or happiness a consumer derives from consuming a good or service. This subjective utility influences economic decisions.
*   **Subjective Nature:** Utility varies from person to person. If two people buy the exact same winter coat, they will not experience the same utility. A person living in a cold climate (Person A) will use it frequently, deriving high utility. A person living in a warm climate (Person B) will rarely use it, deriving low utility.

#### Core Utility Classifications:
1.  **Total Utility (TU):** The overall amount of satisfaction derived from consuming a specific total quantity of a good or service.
2.  **Marginal Utility (MU):** The additional or incremental satisfaction gained from consuming one more unit of a good or service.

#### Mathematical Relationship:
Marginal utility is the change in total utility resulting from a one-unit change in consumption:
$$MU = \frac{\Delta TU}{\Delta Q}$$
$$MU = \frac{TU_{n} - TU_{n-1}}{\Delta Q}$$

### 2. The Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility (LDMU)
The Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility states that as the consumption of a good or service increases, the marginal utility (the additional satisfaction derived from the last unit) decreases.

*   **First Unit:** Derives the highest marginal utility because the consumer's need or hunger is at its peak.
*   **Subsequent Units:** Each additional unit yields less and less satisfaction as the consumer becomes satisfied or full.
*   **Negative Utility:** If consumption continues beyond the point of full satisfaction, marginal utility becomes negative, causing discomfort or dissatisfaction.

<img width="512" height="410" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/a490eddb-1467-43fb-87ba-ce26cfe23829" />

#### Six Essential Assumptions of LDMU:
For this law to hold true, the following conditions must be met:
1.  **Identical Items (Homogeneity):** All units consumed must be identical in size, taste, and quality (e.g., the exact same flavor and brand of ice cream).
2.  **Standard Units:** The units must be of standard size (neither too big nor too small, such as a cup of water, not a single drop or a giant bucket).
3.  **Continuous Consumption:** Consumption must take place continuously in a single time frame without any time breaks or gaps.
4.  **Rational Consumer:** The consumer is assumed to be rational, seeking to maximize utility and capable of making calculated decisions.
5.  **Measurability:** Utility can be measured and quantified.
6.  **Constant Taste and Preferences:** The consumer's taste, habits, and preferences remain constant during the consumption process.

#### Business Application: Persuasion and Pricing
Because marginal utility diminishes, consumers are unwilling to pay the same price for additional units of a good. To persuade customers to purchase more, businesses must decrease the cost per unit. This directly explains multi-unit pricing strategies:
*   *Example:* A store prices 1 backpack at ₹300, 2 backpacks at ₹550, and 3 backpacks at ₹750. The first backpack is priced highest because the consumer needs at least one (high utility). The price per unit is discounted for subsequent units to align with the consumer's diminishing marginal utility.

### 3. Case Study: Chennai Aviation Delay Management
The Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility is heavily used in services to manage customer psychology during operational failures. Consider a flight delay at Chennai airport:

| Duration of Delay | Unit of Compensation Offered | Consumer's Marginal Utility | Total Utility (Consumer's Mental State) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **2 Hours** | Initial Meal (Food and Water) | **High** | Stabilized, frustration is controlled. |
| **4 Hours** | Coffee and Cookies | **Moderate to Low** | Flat; customer begins to get upset. |
| **7 Hours** | ₹1,000 Future Discount Coupon | **Zero** | Customer does not care about future discounts; wants to go home. |
| **10 Hours** | Standard Apologies | **Negative (Destruction Zone)** | Customer becomes hostile. Customer Lifetime Value (CLV) is destroyed. |

#### Tactical Insight for Data Managers:
Once a service delay passes the "Pivot Point" (around 7 hours), marginal utility turns negative, entering the "destruction zone" where Customer Lifetime Value is permanently lost. To recover from service failures, organizations must proactively offer high-utility compensation (such as hotel accommodation) before the pivot point is breached.

### 4. The Water-Diamond Paradox (Paradox of Value)
The Water-Diamond Paradox historical puzzle highlights the conflict between utility and value: why is water, which is essential for survival, so cheap, while diamonds, which are non-essential luxuries, are so expensive?

The Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility resolves this paradox:
*   **Water:** Is highly abundant. Because consumers consume huge quantities of it, the marginal utility of the last cup of water is extremely low. Therefore, its market price (which is determined by marginal utility and scarcity) is very low, even though its total utility is infinitely high.
*   **Diamonds:** Are extremely scarce. Because their supply is limited, consumers consume very few of them, keeping the marginal utility of the last diamond extremely high. Therefore, their market price is very high, even though their total utility is low compared to water.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Market price is determined by marginal utility and scarcity, not by total utility or biological necessity.

## 4.4: Applied Business Case Studies and Numerical Optimizations

To bridge the gap between economic theory and business operations, Business Data Managers apply mathematical optimization models.

### 1. Fleet Optimization (GreenFleet001 Chennai)
A fleet delivery startup manages two types of electric vehicles with a strict daily battery-charging budget cap of ₹20,000.

#### Constants and Parameters:
*   **Daily Charging Cost:**
    *   Electric 2-Wheeler ($E_2$): ₹100/day
    *   Electric 3-Wheeler ($E_3$): ₹400/day
*   **Daily Net Profit:**
    *   Electric 2-Wheeler ($E_2$): ₹500/day
    *   Electric 3-Wheeler ($E_3$): ₹1,500/day
*   **Operational Constraint:** Due to a mandatory B2B contract, the startup must deploy exactly 10 Electric 3-Wheelers ($E_3 = 10$) every day.

#### Step-by-Step Optimization:
**Step 1: Formulate the Daily Budget Constraint Equation**
$$100E_2 + 400E_3 \le 20,000$$

**Step 2: Substitute the fixed constraint ($E_3 = 10$) into the budget equation**
$$100E_2 + 400(10) \le 20,000$$
$$100E_2 + 4,000 \le 20,000$$
$$100E_2 \le 16,000$$
$$E_2 \le 160 \text{ vehicles}$$

To fully utilize the remaining budget and maximize profits, the manager can optimally deploy exactly **160 Electric 2-Wheelers**.

**Step 3: Calculate the Maximum Daily Profit**
$$\text{Total Profit} = (E_2 \times \text{Profit per } E_2) + (E_3 \times \text{Profit per } E_3)$$
$$\text{Total Profit} = (160 \times 500) + (10 \times 1,500)$$
$$\text{Total Profit} = 80,000 + 15,000 = ₹95,000$$
The maximum daily profit achievable under these operational constraints is **₹95,000**.

### 2. Opportunity Cost and Economic Cost Analysis (Data001 Startup)
A growing tech-based software startup has limited resources and must decide how to deploy its team over the next 6 months. Team bandwidth is limited, so they can only commit to one of two mutually exclusive options.

*   **Option A: Prioritize a Time-Sensitive Client Contract**
    *   Data001 will earn an immediate, one-time revenue payment of ₹5.0 Lakh.
    *   However, they must delay a critical internal software upgrade, which requires paying a vendor extension penalty of ₹1.5 Lakh.
*   **Option B: Prioritize the Internal Software Upgrade**
    *   Completing the upgrade immediately reduces the company's operational expenses by ₹10,000 per month.
    *   However, they will not have the bandwidth to take the client contract and will forfeit the ₹5.0 Lakh client contract revenue.

#### Step-by-Step Economic Cost Deconstruction:
If the company decides to choose **Option A** (client contract):

1.  **Opportunity Cost of Option A:** The foregone benefits of the delayed internal upgrade.
    *   Monthly savings foregone $= ₹10,000 / \text{month}$.
    *   Over 6 months, the opportunity cost (Implicit Cost) is:
        $$\text{Implicit Cost} = 6 \text{ months} \times ₹10,000/\text{month} = ₹60,000 \text{ (₹0.60 Lakh)}$$
2.  **Explicit Cost of Option A:** Direct, out-of-pocket expenses incurred.
    *   Vendor extension penalty $= ₹1.50 \text{ Lakh}$.
3.  **Total Economic Cost of Option A:**
    $$\text{Total Economic Cost} = \text{Explicit Cost} + \text{Implicit Cost}$$
    $$\text{Total Economic Cost} = ₹1.50 \text{ Lakh} + ₹0.60 \text{ Lakh} = ₹2.10 \text{ Lakh}$$
The total economic cost of choosing Option A over the 6-month period is **₹2.10 Lakh**.

### 3. Production Frontier and Cost Analysis (Ramu's Tea Stall at IIT Madras)
Ramu operates a food stall near the Himalaya Mess at IIT Madras with a fixed set of resources (stall size, two burners, labor hours, supply of milk, tea leaves, and coffee powder). His Production Possibility Frontier (PPF) represents the maximum output combinations of Tea and Filter Coffee he can produce.

*   **PPF Inefficiency:** If Ramu is producing at a coordinate strictly inside his PPF, it indicates that his stall is underutilizing its available resources or suffering from operational inefficiency.
*   **Average Fixed Cost (AFC) dynamics:** Ramu pays a monthly fixed rent of ₹3,000 for his stall space. Last month, bad weather halved his sales volume.
    *   $\text{Average Fixed Cost (AFC)} = \text{Total Fixed Cost (TFC)} / \text{Quantity (Q)}$.
    *   Because TFC (₹3,000) remained unchanged while $Q$ was cut in half, Ramu's **AFC per cup exactly doubled**.
*   **Average Variable Cost (AVC) Calculation:** Ramu produces 200 cups of tea per day ($Q = 200$). His Total Cost (TC) is ₹15,000, of which ₹8,000 is Fixed Cost (FC).
    *   $\text{Total Variable Cost (TVC)} = \text{Total Cost (TC)} - \text{Fixed Cost (FC)}$
    *   $TVC = ₹15,000 - ₹8,000 = ₹7,000$
    *   $\text{Average Variable Cost (AVC)} = TVC / Q$
    *   $AVC = ₹7,000 / 200 = ₹35/\text{cup}$
Ramu's Average Variable Cost at this level of production is **₹35 per cup**.
